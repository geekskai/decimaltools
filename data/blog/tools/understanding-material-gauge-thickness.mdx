Gauge is not one universal thickness scale. A gauge number must be interpreted with its material convention: sheet steel, galvanized steel, stainless steel, aluminum, and wire systems can assign different thicknesses to the same number. Always identify the material and table before treating a gauge number as a dimension.

For example, the DecimalTools reference table gives these nominal values for **20 gauge**:

| Material convention | Nominal thickness (inches) | Converted thickness (mm, approximate) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sheet steel | 0.0359 | 0.912 |
| Galvanized steel | 0.0396 | 1.006 |

Caltrans's [2019 Standard Steel Product Designation, Table 11.2.3](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/engineering/documents/bridgedesigndetails/chapter-11/2019june112-standard-steel-product-designationa11y.pdf#page=4) lists the same 20-gauge values: 0.0359 inch as uncoated nominal thickness and 0.0396 inch as galvanized nominal thickness. Its notes use the uncoated nominal value for dimensioning material galvanized after fabrication and the galvanized nominal value for dimensioning pre-galvanized material. These are values within that table's convention, not universal purchase requirements or guarantees of manufacturing tolerance.

The [Engineering ToolBox gauge chart](https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/gauge-sheet-d_915.html) also presents separate columns for sheet steel, galvanized steel, stainless steel, and aluminum, illustrating why the material convention matters. Confirm which standard a supplier or drawing uses before comparing values.

The millimeter values above are rounded to three decimal places using 25.4 mm per inch. The two nominal entries differ by about **0.094 mm**. This calculation only compares the entries in the selected table; it does not establish which material or specification applies to a real part.

## How to use a gauge table

1. Identify the material named by the drawing or product documentation.
2. Select the matching gauge convention in the reference table.
3. Read the nominal thickness and its unit; convert inches to millimeters only when useful. The conversion is $1\text{ inch} = 25.4\text{ mm}$.
4. Compare that nominal value with the supplier's specified tolerance and the actual measured part when fit matters.

The unit conversion changes how a dimension is expressed; it does not resolve which gauge standard applies. Likewise, two tables may use different conventions or rounding. Do not combine values from different columns just because the gauge number matches.

For steel sheet, measurement technique is a separate question from choosing the chart convention. ASTM's [A1073/A1073M practice](https://store.astm.org/a1073_a1073m-21.html) describes procedures for measuring uncoated and coated steel sheet with hand micrometers, for products under its committees' jurisdiction when designated by the purchase order or contract. That scope does not make it a universal gauge conversion chart; use the specification named for the material and purchase.

## Gauge is not a tolerance

A gauge chart provides a reference thickness, not a promise that every sheet measures exactly that value. Product specifications may define permissible variation, coating, temper, or other material conditions. The acceptable tolerance depends on the product and its application. For a critical fit or safety requirement, use the material supplier's specification and an appropriate measurement process rather than treating a general chart as a certification.
